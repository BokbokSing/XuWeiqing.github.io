Abstract

    Voice  disorders  are  abnormal  vocalizations  caused by  structural,  functional  or

neurological factors. They can lead to marked vocal impairment like temporary aphonia and

often require long-term rehabilitation and corrective therapy. However, conventional clinical

voice rehabilitation training mostly relies on the patient's active follow-up visits and auditory

perception scoring from voice therapists, which leads to problems such as low patient

compliance and insufficient personalized intervention by doctors. In addition, many patients

tend to overlook the subtle intensity of their voice, which easily leads to overuse of the vocal

cords and affects the recovery process. To address these challenges, a convenient, wearable,

and daily-use voice rehabilitation monitoring and feedback system was designed and developed.

Specifically, a flexible patch-type wearable device was developed, integrating a MEMS

microphone and a triaxial accelerometer to collect the vibration signals and voice signals from

the patient's larynx, enabling continuous monitoring of vocal-related physiological activities.

On this basis, an accompanying mobile software is designed to complete the guidance of user's

rehabilitation behavior. An intelligent voice health status classification model based on

HuBERT  is also built to distinguish between normal and pathological voices. A voice

segmentation and analysis recognition module based on the Whisper model can provide

corresponding acoustic parameters, trend charts, and rehabilitation recommendations. With its

mechanical flexibility, electrical stability, and comfortable design, the wearable system enables

continuous and reliable data collection. The mobile software performs well in terms of usability

and interaction design, providing monitoring visualizations, rehabilitation plan reminders, and

model-driven intelligent feedback. The model performs stably in voice feature recognition

tasks and achieves ideal results in key indicators such as accuracy, recall rate, and confusion

matrix. The system has good  wearability, system  usability, and  intelligent recognition

capabilities, showing potential application value in improving rehabilitation compliance and

supporting personalized care. Moving forward, clinical controlled trials will be carried out, and

the model and software interaction will be further refined to validate the system in real-world

clinical settings and support more precise clinical management.

Chapter 1 Introduction

1.1 Background

    Voice disorders are abnormalities in phonation caused by  structural, functional or

neurological factors. Epidemiological studies have shown that almost 30% of the adult

population experience voice problems at some point in their lives [1]. Professionals such as

teachers, singers, customer service staff, and broadcasters, which require a high degree of

reliance on the voice, are particularly prone to this problem [2]. Just as shown in the scenario

of Figure 1, voice disorders not only directly affect communication efficiency, but also may

cause persistent psychological stress and occupational problems, and even lead to social

isolation, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life [3-6]. Therefore, how to carry out

effective and individualized voice rehabilitation management  is an important issue that

urgently needs to be addressed in the field of health.





Figure 1. Representative user scenario of voice disorder.
     In recent years, wearable technology and artificial intelligence(AI) have been rapidly

developed and applied in the medical field, creating conditions for the implementation of long-

term, precise, personalized and remotely accessible voice rehabilitation management [7-11].

Modern wearable devices can continuously collect voice and motion data with minimal burden

in daily environments [12]. Audio signal processing and machine learning technologies are

capable of automatically extracting quantitative acoustic features reflecting voice states from

time series data and identifying patterns related to voice function or disorders [13-16].

Combining wearable technology and data-driven algorithms can achieve real-time monitoring

of patients’ voice usage, quantitative assessment based on data, personalized training methods

and telemedicine support.

1.2 Challenges

    However, to truly transform the system based on wearable technology and AI into a voice

rehabilitation solution that can be used in clinical practice, multiple challenges need to be

addressed, including sensor, data acquisition, algorithms, system integration and interaction

design.

    At present, most rehabilitation plans are more based on doctor’s clinical experience.

Although they can make certain personal adjustments according to the differences in voice

characteristics of different patients, this process mainly relies on qualitative judgment and

intermittent follow-up visits, lacking a long-term tracking and precise feedback mechanism

based on dynamic objective data. Figure 2 illustrates the traditional rehabilitation process from

voice impairment to laryngeal recovery training. Clinical rehabilitation mainly relies on a small

amount of inpatient treatment and nearly fixed rehabilitation training prescriptions.





Figure 2. Clinical rehabilitation relies on infrequent hospital visits.
     Current voice assessment often relies on specialized facilities such as speech laboratories,

laryngoscopes, and acoustic acquisition rooms. These facilities are costly and not easily

accessible, and assessments must be conducted in hospitals. Therefore, assessment frequency

is low, patient compliance is limited, and the results may not capture real changes across time

and different daily situations. It limits the refined adjustment of rehabilitation strategies and

the long-term effect evaluation.

    Although commercial microphones and  portable  recorders  are widely  accessible,

dedicated equipment for clinical voice rehabilitation remains immature [17, 18]. To collect

valuable voice data for diagnosis and treatment in a daily environment, several engineering

challenges must be addressed, including environmental noise [9], wearing comfort, battery

capacity and data bandwidth requirements [19]. At the same time, the device design must
                                               9

===== SOURCE PAGE 11 =====
balance sensitivity, privacy protection, and usability requirements [20, 21].

    Although current wearable devices can generate a large amount of health data, most

applications only provide raw data or simple trend visualizations, and are unable to transform

into proactive, personalized and reliable health guidance [22].

    About algorithm, there have been extensive research results in speech recognition,

acoustic feature engineering, and disease identification based on machine learning, which can

classify or score vocal disorders under controlled conditions [23, 24]. However, when exposed

to noisy data from different devices and environments, the model often suffers from domain

shift and poor generalization, leading to unstable performance across patients and scenarios

and hindering reliable use in real rehabilitation settings [9]. Furthermore, the quantity of

clinical annotations  is  limited, and the evaluation  results are susceptible to individual

experience  differences,  resulting  in low consistency  in  annotations. Consequently,  the

effectiveness  and  generalization  of  supervised  learning  approaches  are  significantly

constrained [25-27]. More importantly, the output of the algorithm often lacks a direct mapping

with clinical diagnosis and patient behavior changes, resulting in a lack of a data-based

feedback loop in the rehabilitation process. This makes it difficult to continuously promote the

optimization of patients' vocalization behavior in real-life situations [28, 29].

1.3 Research gap

     In recent years, studies have attempted to apply wearable technology, acoustic signal

analysis, and machine learning methods to the context of voice rehabilitation and have verified

their potential in behavior tracking and disease identification through short-term experiments

[30]. However, there is still a significant lack of research on how to systematically apply these

technologies in real-world, long-term rehabilitation settings. Specifically, there are three key

research gaps in literature.

     (1) There is still a scarcity of research on integrating wearable hardware, real-time signal

         processing, and machine learning models at the system level.

     (2)  Patient-centered personalized rehabilitation interventions have not been fully realized.

     (3) There is a lack of data-driven evaluation mechanisms that cover long time series and

          natural contexts for data collection and closed-loop operations.

                                              10

===== SOURCE PAGE 12 =====
     Overall, the existing technical routes still lack systematicity in the process of transitioning

from experimental validation to long-term rehabilitation and clinical applications.

1.4 Significance

    Given that the current research still has limitations in the long-term application and

personalized intervention of wearable voice rehabilitation systems, promoting the research and

practice of such intelligent systems holds significant clinical value and social significance.

    For patients, they often have difficulty accurately assessing their own vocal usage. In daily

life, they may unconsciously overuse their voices or make improper vocalizations [31], thereby

prolonging the recovery period or even causing secondary damage. Through real-time

monitoring and personalized feedback mechanisms, patients can gain awareness of their vocal

behavior and make timely adjustments, avoiding unnecessary vocal strain and completing

rehabilitation training more safely.

    Voice rehabilitation usually relies on patients' subjective feedback and regular follow-ups.

However, this approach has certain limitations. For instance, patients' subjective feedback may

not be precise enough, making it difficult for doctors to accurately assess the recovery progress.

Doctors' reliance on manual judgment may introduce certain experience biases, potentially

affecting the optimization of treatment plans. The long follow-up period may prevent issues

from being adjusted in a timely manner, thereby impacting the final rehabilitation outcome.

    The traditional voice rehabilitation methods also increase the burden on patients' visits

and the workload of medical institutions. For regions with strained medical resources, patients

may have difficulty obtaining professional rehabilitation guidance in a timely manner, resulting

in restricted rehabilitation processes. For the medical system, remote monitoring and self-

rehabilitation mechanisms can help alleviate the pressure of follow-up visits, improve resource

utilization efficiency, and enhance the accessibility of rehabilitation services in terms of time

and location.

    From a broader perspective, such intelligent rehabilitation systems are expected to

facilitate the transition of voice medicine from an experience-driven model to a data-driven

one, providing a model for the future individualized medical approach.

1.5 Research goals and contributions

    The overall objective of this study is to develop a modular and clinically applicable

wearable intelligent voice rehabilitation system, enabling continuous real-time monitoring of a

wide range of voice disorders, personalized feedback based on data, and assistance in clinical

decision-making. To achieve this, this study intends to focus on the following goals.

     (1) Design and deploy a smart voice-wearing system that can be worn for a long time,

         has low interference, ensuring the data quality in natural environments.

     (2) Develop modeling and machine learning algorithms for voice data, thereby enabling

         recognition and individualized characterization of improper vocal behaviors.

     (3) Design a closed-loop interaction framework for patients, algorithms, and doctors,

         converting the model outputs into interpretable and executable training suggestions.

    To achieve these goals, this study proposes and implements an intelligent wearable voice

rehabilitation system that integrates hardware acquisition, analysis, and personalized feedback.

The system can stably collect multimodal voice-related signals in natural situations and process

and model them through machine learning algorithms, thereby providing patients with

monitoring and personalized rehabilitation guidance based on data-driven methods.



Chapter 2 Literature Review

2.1 Conventional clinical voice rehabilitation

     Traditional clinical voice therapy is the mainstream approach for treating voice disorders

such as dysphonia. Multiple reviews and systematic studies have shown that behavioral voice

therapy-based programs, including direct vocal exercises, vocal cord relaxation, and semi-

occluded vocal tract (SOVT) exercises, have significant improvements in subjective voice

quality perception, vocal cord vibration stability, acoustic parameters, and patients' quality of

life [32, 33]. Especially for exercises such as voicing based on SOVT, water-resistance therapy

(WRT), and vocal pipe voicing, significant improvements in acoustic parameters such as

fundamental frequency (F0), noise-to-harmonics ratio (NHR), and cepstral peak prominence

(CPP) can be observed in a short period of time, indicating an increase in vocal efficiency, a

reduction in noise components, and more stable vocal cord vibration [33].


                                              12

===== SOURCE PAGE 14 =====
    Recent studies have further demonstrated that there may be significant differences

between the acoustic measurements of patients in a clinical setting and their self-perceived

vocal state in natural usage scenarios, suggesting that the existing assessment systems have

limitations in terms of ecological validity [34]. Meanwhile, Lam et al. pointed out that voice

rehabilitation should  gradually  shift from a  standardized,  therapist-driven model  to a

personalized approach based on continuous data monitoring, tracking of individual change

trajectories, and adaptive feedback, to better meet the actual rehabilitation needs of patients

[35]. Therefore, developing intelligent rehabilitation systems that are capable of real-time

operation, continuity, and individual adaptability has become an important direction for

breaking through the constraints of traditional speech therapy models.

2.2 Software-based voice rehabilitation and voice assistants

    With the advancements in speech recognition, acoustic feature analysis, and machine

learning technologies, a series of studies have verified the feasibility of identifying and grading

voice  disorders  through  automated  acoustic  analysis.  Researchers  use  basic  acoustic

parameters, such as F0,  jitter, shimmer, HNR, CPP,  etc., to classify healthy voices and

pathological voices, thereby providing quantitative evidence for the diagnosis and monitoring

of voice disorders [36, 37]. For instance, the research conducted by Lee, J. B. et al. combined

traditional  features  with machine  learning  classifiers and  reported  a  relatively  high

classification accuracy rate [38]. The application of voice assistant (VA) technology in the field

of health management has also developed rapidly. The current intelligent VA based on large

language models can not only provide health information interpretation and medication

reminders for the elderly or patients with chronic diseases, but also reduce the usage threshold

for patients through conversational interaction, thereby enhancing their compliance and self-

management ability [39, 40].

2.3 Wearable health monitoring for rehabilitation

    Although research  at the software  level has made  significant progress  in speech

recognition and speech interaction, its applications are still mainly focused on algorithm and

interface development, lacking integration with wearable hardware and physiological signal

collection. Due to the inability to obtain continuous data of patients' daily vocal behaviors, such


                                              13

===== SOURCE PAGE 15 =====
systems are difficult to achieve dynamic monitoring and personalized rehabilitation feedback.

     In recent years, research has begun to explore voice-related wearable systems, integrating

sensors such as accelerometers and microphones into smart collars, ear-worn devices, or wrist-

worn devices to record vocal activities in natural settings. These prototype systems have

demonstrated feasibility in aspects such as voice pattern recognition, extraction of vocal cord

vibration characteristics, and detection of excessive vocal usage behaviors, verifying the

research potential of wearable voice monitoring solutions. However, these efforts are still

mostly at the stage of experimental verification. They have not yet met the long-term

rehabilitation requirements in terms of device form, comfort, and signal stability, and lack

systematic clinical validation for real patient populations.

2.4 Flexible integrated circuit

    The traditional hard or flexible printed circuits (FPC) prepared based on Printed Circuit

Board (PCB) technology can ensure reliable electrical pathways, but they can only be bent or

folded, and cannot be stretched or deformed, thus failing to meet the softness required by

human skin. In 2008, Kim et al. first proposed the concept of stretchable and foldable silicon

integrated circuits (IC), providing a prototype framework for stretchable electronics with

extensible interconnection structures and soft-hard integration strategies [41]. In 2016, the

Rogers team proposed epidermal electronics and published it in Science. This system achieves

compatibility with the mechanical properties of the skin through ultra-thin flexible substrates,

serpentine interconnects, and island structures. At the same time, it integrates functions such

as wireless communication, inductive coils, electrocardiogram (ECG), electromyogram (EMG),

strain and temperature sensing, enabling non-invasive monitoring of cardiac activities, muscle

movements and neural signals [42].

    Based on  this, recent research has further expanded the application boundaries of

stretchable  electronics. Several  studies have integrated machine learning methods  into

wearable platforms, demonstrating the feasibility of real-time signal processing based on data-

driven approaches and closed-loop feedback for  rehabilitation purposes  [8, 30]. These

advancements indicate that integrating skin-adhesive devices, machine learning computations,

and personalized feedback is a reasonable approach for constructing an intelligent wearable

system with clinical value for postoperative voice rehabilitation.

    The development of materials science has also accelerated the manufacturing process of

stretchable integrated circuits. For instance, Ai et al. proposed stretchable solder for stretchable

electronic interconnections [43], effectively enhancing the electrical connection reliability of

electronic devices under multi-axis strain.



Chapter 3 Methodology

3.1 Overall system architecture

    This study has designed an intelligent voice rehabilitation system consisting of a flexible

wearable signal collection device that can be directly attached to human skin, a mobile

application (APP), a cloud analysis model, and a PC Host (Figure 3). This architecture aims to

achieve the collection of daily physiological data from users in the recovery period, the analysis

of voice function and status, and the provision of individualized rehabilitation training

assistance.  It provides a complete technical chain for continuous monitoring and remote

rehabilitation management. The complete system is still under continuous development. This

section mainly describes its design principles, mode selection, main sensor selection, and data

flow.





Figure 3. Overall framework diagram of the intelligent wearable system.
    This study will employ Bi-modal signals of speech and acceleration as input features. The

acoustic signal serves as the most crucial analytical basis in the existing clinical diagnostic

system, enabling the system's output to be consistent with the existing acoustic evaluation
                                              15

===== SOURCE PAGE 17 =====
indicators. The acceleration signal can first capture physiological and movement information

related to vocalization behavior, such as respiratory rate (RR), laryngeal muscle vibration

amplitude, and body posture changes, which are closely related to vocal quality and help the

model calibrate and understand the collected speech. The acceleration signal can also play a

supplementary role when the acoustic signal  is interfered with by environmental noise,

achieving continuous and stable data analysis.

     Therefore, to collect the acoustic signals of users and the acceleration signals on the

surface  of  their  necks,  a  flexible  wearable  acquisition  device  integrating  a  triaxial

accelerometer and a microphone was designed and fabricated. This device is composed of a

flexible substrate and electronic components with signal processing functions, connected

through an S-shaped copper wire network, and its packaging process enables lightweight,

continuous and non-invasive monitoring. The raw data collected are initially processed by the

MCU and then transmitted to the smartphone using the MQTT communication protocol.

     Further signal processing is carried out in the mobile APP, including multi-modal feature

extraction, acoustic analysis, real-time voice status monitoring, training record management,

and generation of personalized rehabilitation suggestions. The APP is also responsible for

uploading structured data and voice features to the cloud for long-term trend modeling and

prediction of the patient's rehabilitation status.

    The cloud analysis model is trained based on clinically collected patient data and time-

series data from wearable devices, and can generate automated assessment, risk warnings, and

rehabilitation suggestions tailored to individual recovery.

    The future system also includes a PC Host end for doctors and patients to view long-term

rehabilitation data, historical reports, trend curves, and model prediction results, supporting

remote medical care and clinical decision-making.

3.2 Hardware design and fabrication

3.2.1 Wearable hardware design

    The device designed in this study is a wearable voice data acquisition device based on

acoustic  sensing  and  skin  surface  acceleration  perception.   It   integrates  micro-

electromechanical  system (MEMS)  microphones,  triaxial  accelerometer  (ADXL345),


                                              16

===== SOURCE PAGE 18 =====
microcontrollers (ESP32), wireless communication modules, power management circuits and

flexible interconnection structures, and is used for long-term, continuous and low-burden

collection of multimodal voice data of users.

   MEMS microphones, as the primary acoustic sensor, are used to collect audio signals.

They have a high sampling frequency, capable of covering frequencies far beyond the human

speech range (80–4000 Hz). Their signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is high (typically > 60 dB),

enabling them to effectively resist noise interference in daily environments. Moreover, MEMS

microphones are small, have low power consumption, and are easy to integrate into wearable

devices. Additionally, the audio data can be directly aligned with the clinically collected patient

data, supporting the construction of cloud-based machine learning models and achieving

connection with acoustic parameter analysis. Although the acceleration signal can reflect the

vocal behavior to some extent, due to the influence of soft tissue thickness, fitting degree, and

posture changes, spectral distortion and energy loss are inevitable. Therefore, the audio signal

still serves as the main mode of the system, undertaking the main functions of model training,

acoustic feature extraction, and clinical consistency analysis.

   A triaxial MEMS accelerometer (ADXL345, ±2 g range) was used to supplement the

acoustic recordings. It is a capacitive MEMS accelerometer that detects acceleration by

measuring the capacitance change between fixed electrodes and the suspended structure due to

inertia. The sampling range of ADXL345 is up to 800 Hz, covering the main physiological

vibration frequency bands on the surface of the neck skin. As Figure 5 shows, it captures low-

frequency components, including breathing (0.1–0.8 Hz), heartbeat (0–5 Hz), subtle body

movements (0.5–10 Hz), and vocal-induced vibrations (90–2000 Hz). The acceleration signal

is mainly used for two purposes: one is to help separate some non-speech components closely

related to speech rehabilitation; the other is to assist in identifying speech events and health

status in the presence of external noise pollution, thereby improving the robustness of the

system. The schematic diagram of the acceleration signal acquisition module is shown in

Figure 4. At the SDA and SCL ports, each was equipped with a 10k resistor to improve the

3.2.3 Flexible circuit design

    This section implements the functions of the traditional rigid circuit board in Section 3.2.1

through flexible circuits. The realization of flexible circuits requires arranging the stretchable

electronic circuit structures on a flexible substrate with low elastic modulus, using S-shaped

conductive lines to connect the core rigid chip and passive components, and locally encapsulate

high modulus elastic materials to prevent failure due to breakage at the connection points.

Finally, an integrated and modular packaging is carried out.

     Stretchable electronic devices can be bent and fit onto complex curved surfaces, such as

the surface of the human body [44]. Here, the first step is to make the rigid electronic circuits

stretchable. Inspired by the serpentine interconnect strategy proposed by Kim et al. [41, 44],

all conductive traces were designed in a S-shape and arranged in CAD according to the

alignment and dimensional constraints of the IC and passive components (Figure 7).

copper traces that serve as stretchable interconnects. The S-shaped copper traces enable

controlled deformation under stretching or bending, effectively redistributing mechanical strain

away from the rigid components and ensuring both  electrical  stability and mechanical

3.2.4 Selection of materials for flexible circuits

    The performance of flexible circuits is closely related to the flexible materials and metal

materials used. To ensure the reliability and performance of the circuits, we thoroughly

analyzed  the  advantages  and  disadvantages  of  different  materials,  comprehensively

considering the elastic modulus, heat resistance, tensile properties and other characteristics of

the materials. Finally, we selected the most suitable materials for the fabrication of flexible

circuits.

    Copper  is a low-cost and high-modulus conductive material with  stable material

properties, low susceptibility to oxidation, and ease of laser etching. Therefore, copper is

chosen as the material for the S-shaped wires.

    The substrate of the copper wire was selected as a film-like polyimide (PI) with excellent

bending and stretching resistance. The PI material has excellent thermal stability and can

usually withstand temperatures above 250℃, making it suitable for use in electronic devices

that may generate heat. PI is also an electrical insulator, which can effectively prevent current

leakage or short circuits. The most important aspect is that PI has good adhesion to the copper

material. During the manufacturing process, the PI film can maintain a stable bond with the

copper wire, ensuring that the copper substrate can easily return to its initial state during the

stretching process. Moreover, through appropriate surface treatment, the adhesion strength

between the PI film and the copper wire can be further enhanced [46].

    Table 2 lists the properties of several commonly used flexible packaging materials.

Through comparative analysis, Eco-flex elastomer has an extremely low elastic modulus. It is

very  soft, has high conformability, can perfectly  fit the skin, and  is  resistant to high

temperatures. These three properties indicate that it can be used as a packaging material for

circuits. Secondly, various connection points of flexible circuits are prone to breakage. To

prevent this from happening, the packaging material needs to meet certain high elastic modulus

requirements. And high-modulus PDMS can precisely achieve the protection of the connection

positions of the chips.

3.2.5 The fabrication process flow

    As shown in the Figure 9, the typical manufacturing process[30] of wireless flexible

electronics usually includes the following  steps: (1)  laser cutting to form a S-shaped

interconnection [47], (2) water-soluble transfer to move the rigid electronic components to the

flexible substrate [48], (3) low-temperature reflow soldering to complete the component

mounting and interconnection, and (4) integrated packaging with soft elastic polymers to

provide flexible packaging and skin compatibility [49].





Figure 9. Fabrication process of the flexible device.

===== SOURCE PAGE 26 =====
      First, a 6×6 cm² rigid glass substrate needs to be prepared. Apply the prepared PDMS at

a speed of 600 r/min on the glass plate for 10 seconds and then cure it at 65 ℃ for 2 hours to

obtain a glass sheet covered with PDMS film. Next, a 100 μm-thick copper-coated PI film was

hot-pressed onto the PDMS film, followed by heating to 120 °C to improve adhesion between

the two layers.

     Place the fixed copper foil under a 355nm nanosecond laser. Input the contour drawing of

the stretchable S-shaped structure network designed in Auto CAD. Adjust the power of the blue

light to 10W and the cutting speed to 200 mm/min for 10 cycles of cutting. After all the contours

have been cut, use forceps to remove the remaining copper foil. Then, use a dust-free cotton

swab dipped in alcohol to clean the oxide residues left by the laser etching at the edges of the

conductive network, thereby obtaining the pre-designed conductive copper-based circuit.

     Next, use the adhesive surface of a water-soluble tape to adhere the prepared complete S-

shaped copper conductive circuit, so that it sticks to the water-soluble tape. An ultralow

elastic silicone film, Ecoflex, with a thickness of 1mm and a size of 6×6 cm² was prepared by

spin-coating. PDMS and the curing agent were mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio to form the Ecoflex.

Before it isn’t fully cured, the S-shaped copper conductive wires and the adhesive tape are

adhered to one side of the Ecoflex film, and then hot pressing was carried out to promote the

adhesion between the PI and the Ecoflex film.

    Then, the Ecoflex with the water-soluble adhesive tape was placed in deionized water for

about 8 hours to completely dissolve the water-soluble adhesive tape. After that, the residual

water-soluble adhesive tape fibers on the surface were washed with deionized water, and the

residual moisture around the entire conductive structure was dried using a hot air blower. Thus,

a transplanted conductive network was obtained.

   A 6×6 cm², 30 μm-thick PET film was laser-etched using a 355 nm laser. Rectangular

openings matching the chip footprint and the conductive areas susceptible to short-circuiting

were created, resulting in a PET masking layer. The PET mask was subsequently aligned and

bonded to the conductive network via van der Waals adhesion to the silicone. Apply a layer of

soldering agent on the surface of the PET mask and apply an appropriate amount of low

temperature reflow solder paste. Then use a scraper to level it out so that the solder paste is

===== SOURCE PAGE 27 =====
placed at each welding position. Next, peel off the PET mask and place the electronic

components at the corresponding pins. Place the entire glass plate with circuits and components

into the reflow soldering box. Set the heating temperature at 150 ℃ and the heating time at 10

     Place the soldered circuit in a plasma treatment furnace and treat it at a power of 100W

3.2.6 The selection of the wearing position

    The larynx is the core structure for vocalization. The vibration of the vocal cords and

related physiological activities will generate measurable mechanical vibration signals on the

skin surface [42]. In order to determine the most optimal area for wearing the sensor, and to

achieve the most reasonable and accurate monitoring of the patient's vocal state, this study

referred to the previous research results on skin-adhesive and throat-wearable sensing systems

[8, 30, 45, 50], and chose to attach the sensor to the front of the neck, on the skin surface

corresponding to the structure of the larynx. Considering the differences in the soft tissue

structure of the neck, the position and thickness of the thyroid cartilage with respect to height,

some studies have divided this skin area into three possible attachment positions: lower (A),

middle (B), and upper (C) (Figure 10a). They have explored the potential impact of these

different attachment positions on signal quality [30, 50]. Research shows that in the heart rate

(HR) monitoring task, due to the weak vibration in the z-axis direction caused by the heart's

beating, the normal component displacement of the vibration did not change. Therefore, the

HR that decouples with the position shift is not affected by the change in position. While in the

RR monitoring task, the lung breathing pushes the chest ribs to move upward along the y-axis

(Figure 10b). The vibration of the sensor decreases in amplitude as the position rises, and the

accelerometer reflects a decrease in respiratory intensity, so the effect of decoupled RR is

significantly reduced.


                                              26

===== SOURCE PAGE 28 =====
Figure 10. Illustration of sensor placement and accelerometer axis definition. a, Three
potential placement regions on the anterior neck corresponding to the laryngeal area (lower,
middle, upper). b, Orientation of the triaxial accelerometer.
    Based on the above signal propagation mechanism, it can be inferred that position A is

theoretically more conducive to capturing the original acceleration signal that contains more

physiological vibration signals. Especially in cases where respiratory signals or low-frequency

physiological activities need to be monitored, its advantage becomes even more prominent.

    However, in the context of wearable device design, the position of the device also needs

to consider factors such as comfort, user acceptance,  stability during daily wear, and

appearance. Position A is lower, which is more likely to be affected by the collar or skin folds,

and the user may also experience a sense of foreign objects. In contrast, position B is located

near the thyroid cartilage, which can maintain a better signal quality and is less likely to cause

motion interference and wearing discomfort.

     Therefore, considering both factors, this study ultimately chose position B as the standard

wearing position, and further verified the overall balance between signal quality and wearable

experience in the subsequent experiments.

3.7 Machine learning algorithms for classification and prediction

3.7.1 Model Overview

    To achieve the binary classification task of normal and pathological voices based on voice

signals, this study adopts a pre-trained self-supervised speech model to construct a voice

recognition framework. The core structure of the system is based on HuBERT as the backbone

network for acoustic feature extraction, which is used to extract high-level time-frequency

structure features from short-time speech segments. On this basis, the output layer of the

original model  is replaced with a single-layer linear  classifier and adjusted to a two-

dimensional output according to the task setting, corresponding to the labels of Normal voice

and Pathological voice. Finally, the model generates classification probabilities through

Softmax for sample-level prediction and subsequent patient-level decision analysis. The model

structure is shown in Figure 18.
                                              34

===== SOURCE PAGE 36 =====
Figure 18. The overview of the voice recognition model using HuBERT as the main feature
extraction network.

3.7.2 Model Architecture

     Specifically, HuBERT-Base is adopted as the feature extractor, with all the Transformer-

based encoder layers frozen and only the additional linear classification head being fine-tuned.

The model consists of an input signal processing module, the HuBERT pre-trained acoustic

feature backbone network, a pooling layer, and a linear classification head. The overall

structure is shown in the Figure 19. The Adam optimizer is used, with an initial learning rate

of 1×10⁻⁵ to ensure a stable training process and avoid overfitting. The training period is set at

25 epochs, with a batch size of 16.





Figure 19. End-to-end fine-tuning pipeline for self-supervised speech models (HuBERT).
    The model input is the original speech waveform sampled at 16 kHz. To reduce the

influence of differences in the recording environment and improve the model's adaptability, as

well as to provide a consistent signal distribution for the feature extraction stage, the input

signal needs to be preprocessed. The specific method is to standardize, truncate silent parts,

and perform data augmentation on the input signal before it enters the backbone network.

    The backbone network adopts HuBERT-base as the speech representation model. Its

                                              35

===== SOURCE PAGE 37 =====
structure consists of two modules, including the convolutional feature encoder and the

Transformer stacking layers. The Feature Encoder converts the input waveform into continuous

audio features, capturing the short-term speech structure. The Transformer Layers further

model the global dependencies across the time  series, generating a high-level speech

embedding of dimension 𝑇× 𝐷, which can reflect deep features such as speaking behavior

patterns. During the training process, HuBERT’s parameters are kept frozen to avoid overfitting

on the limited-scale medical speech data.

    The time-series signals output by HuBERT are compressed through the pooling module

to generate fixed-length global speech representations. The pooling method employs the mean

pooling structure, enabling the model to aggregate sentence-level statistical information rather

than relying on local features. Subsequently, the pooled output is sent to a single-layer linear

classification head, which is responsible for mapping the feature vectors to the task category

space. Finally, the output is transformed into a probability distribution through the Softmax

activation function, which is used for subsequent prediction and evaluation.

3.7.4 Training strategy and Hyperparameters

    This study adopts the transfer learning strategy, using the pre-trained speech model

HuBERT as the base model, and combines methods such as data augmentation, imbalance

learning mechanism, accuracy optimization, and patient-level prediction fusion to enhance the

robustness and generalization ability of the model in clinical speech data scenarios.

    The implementation leverages Hugging Face’s AutoModelForAudioClassification to load

the pre-trained HuBERT model weights. To prevent the model from overfitting under the

condition of limited labeled samples, only the parameters of the classification head are updated

during the training stage. The HuBERT encoder part remains in a frozen state.

    To enhance the model's robustness to differences in recording environments and variations

in vocalization, this study employed a dynamic data augmentation strategy during the training

phase and applied it to the training set. The augmentation strategy consisted of two methods:

adding white noise and time scaling. Specifically, a random noise with an amplitude of 0.005

was injected into the speech signal with a 50% probability to simulate the underlying noise

distribution in real recording environments. Additionally, the speech playback rate was

randomly adjusted with a 30% probability, ranging from 0.9 times to 1.1 times. Linear

interpolation was used for resampling to increase the model's tolerance for differences in

speech speed and rhythm.

    Due to the uneven distribution of categories in medical speech data, this study employs

Weighted Random Sampling to alleviate the category bias during the training process. Firstly,

the category balance weights are calculated based on the number of samples in each category.

Then, these weights are passed to the WeightedRandomSampler to ensure that the model

receives a relatively balanced distribution of category samples at the mini-batch level, thereby

enhancing the recall ability of rare categories and the overall F1-score performance.

    During the training process, the standard cross-entropy loss function was used for

optimization. Since the data imbalance issue has been handled in the sampling stage, no

additional weighting was applied to the loss function.

    The optimization strategy employs the Adam optimizer and combines multiple scheduling

mechanisms to enhance training efficiency and model convergence performance, namely

learning rate scheduling, early stopping strategy, and mixed precision training. In terms of
                                              37

===== SOURCE PAGE 39 =====
learning rate scheduling, the cosine decay strategy is adopted, and 10% of the training steps

are set as Warmup to reduce initial gradient oscillations. During the model training process, the

early stopping strategy is used, and the macro-average F1-score is used as the monitoring

indicator. If this indicator does not improve for 6 consecutive epochs, the training is stopped to

avoid overfitting. Half-precision calculation is utilized to accelerate the training process and

reduce memory usage, making the model training more efficient.

    Considering that a single audio segment may not fully represent the patient's overall voice

condition, the reasoning stage adopts Patient-level Aggregation. The model first calculates the

logits of all slices for the same patient, averages them, and then performs classification

determination.

3.7.5 Evaluation Metrics

    To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the model in the voice rehabilitation

classification  task,  this study employs multi-dimensional  evaluation  metrics, including

Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score. Accuracy is used to measure the proportion of

correctly  classified samples among  all predictions of the model, reflecting the overall

classification performance. However, in scenarios where the distribution of data categories is

imbalanced, Accuracy may mask the model's insufficient ability to identify minority classes.

To more accurately measure the classification quality, this study further introduces other

indicators. Among them, Precision measures the proportion of samples predicted to belong to

a certain category among the actual samples belonging to that category, used to assess the

model's  false alarm  situation.  Recall measures  the  proportion  of  true  target samples

successfully detected by the model, used to assess the missed detection situation. F1-score is

the harmonic average of Precision and Recall, more suitable for scenarios with imbalanced

categories, and can comprehensively reflect the classification performance of the model.

    To ensure that each category has the same weight in the evaluation process and is not

affected by  the sample  quantity,  all  indicators  are  calculated using macro averaging.

Additionally, in the testing phase, all evaluation indicators are calculated on the “patient-level

aggregated inference” to be closer to the recognition performance in real clinical application

scenarios.

breathing are mostly around 1 Hz, and those related to the heartbeat are mostly around 5 Hz.

The specific frequency bands need to be further verified through analysis of the original signals.

    The subjects were invited to wear the device and perform various actions such as holding

4.3.2 Analysis of audio signals

     In  this study, the acoustic features of  clinical speech data were analyzed. Using

representative male samples, we compared healthy individuals with patients diagnosed with

four common voice disorders: vocal cord cyst, laryngeal edema, vocal cord polyps, and vocal

nodules. The analyzed features included Jitter, Shimmer, HNR, CPP, Vocal Range, F0 SD, and

Singing Power Ratio (SPR). These parameters collectively represent the stability of the sound

source, source quality and functional acoustic performance.

    As shown  in Figure  26(a-d),  Jitter, Shimmer, HNR and CPP exhibit  consistent

differentiation patterns in terms of source stability between healthy and pathological voices.

Jitter reflects the periodic fluctuations of F0 and is the core indicator of frequency stability.

Healthy individuals show the lowest Jitter, while structural lesions (such as polyps and cysts)

increase due to their impact on vocal cord closure and the regularity of periodic vibration.

Shimmer describes amplitude stability and reflects the consistency of vocal cord vibration

energy output. Like  Jitter, pathological samples show a significant increase, suggesting

insufficient airflow leakage and amplitude regulation during vocalization. HNR represents the

ratio of harmonic to noise energy and is an important signal of sound source quality. Healthy

voices have a higher HNR, while pathological voices significantly decrease due to disordered

vibration and increased turbulence, which is associated with hoarseness. CPP is an objective

indicator that has been widely used in clinical practice in recent years, which can quantify the

significance of F0 components in the cephalogram domain. Figure 26d shows that healthy

voice samples have a higher CPP, while damaged vocal cord structures lead to a significant

decrease in CPP, which is related to hoarseness.

    As shown in Figure 26(e-f), apart from the stability of the sound source, there are also

differences in the functional acoustic performance. Vocal Range represents the effective pitch

range that can be produced. Healthy individuals generally exhibit a wider vocal range, while

vocal cord disorders limit the vibration boundaries, resulting in a narrowed vocal range. F0 SD

reflects the dynamic ability of pitch changes during speech production. Healthy samples show
                                              48

===== SOURCE PAGE 50 =====
a higher fluctuation amplitude, while pathological speech tends to be monotonous or restricted.

     Finally, as can be seen from Figure 26g, the discriminatory power of SPR is relatively

limited, suggesting that this parameter may have insufficient sensitivity for structural vocal

cord lesions, or  it may be more valuable for diagnosis only in specific vocal production

situations, such as singing.

Chapter 5 Discussion

5.1 Limitations

    Although this thesis has made certain progress in the research of wearable devices,

analysis models, and system integration, due to practical, design, and technical limitations

caused by factors such as time constraints and difficulties in conducting clinical experiments,

there are still some issues that require further research and improvement.

    The hardware produced is merely a data collection device. Currently, it needs to interact

with the designed mobile application software. Therefore, the device still needs to improve in

terms of user interface friendliness and intelligence. Moreover, in terms of device size, the

selected MCU chip has the drawback of slightly larger volume. In daily use, there are problems

such as inconvenient battery external connection and small capacity. And although the mobile

application has verified its usability, issues related to privacy protection, continuous interaction

experience, and long-term compliance in actual clinical use scenarios  still need further

verification and optimization.

    The current hardware has integrated audio and accelerometer sensors, but there is still

                                              52

===== SOURCE PAGE 54 =====
potential for further reduction in overall size. Future iterations may enhance the wearable

nature and long-term comfort of the device by using smaller and more advanced MCUs and

reducing the number of sensors. Existing research [54-57] indicates that there is a high

correlation between the neck vibration patterns based on accelerometers and acoustic speech

features, meaning that a single-modal system relying solely on accelerometers may also be

feasible. However, if relying solely on acceleration signals, two issues need to be addressed:

Firstly, there is currently a lack of a mature mapping system from acceleration signals to clinical

acoustic  indicators, which cannot  directly correspond  to  the commonly used acoustic

assessment standards in hospitals. Secondly, the feature dimensions and interpretability

extractable from acceleration signals are not as good as those from speech signals. Therefore,

although the dual-modal acquisition function of the hardware has been achieved, adopting a

dual-modal  strategy remains a reasonable choice  for balancing  reliability and  clinical

application feasibility. However, cross-modal feature fusion algorithms and real-time reasoning

mechanisms are still in the early stages of development.

    This study has revealed through analysis that the vibration signals received by the

acceleration sensor can separate out the RR, HR, and SW. However, these physiological factors

have not yet been incorporated into the classification and recognition model. At the same time,

the scale of the training data for the existing model and the participant population are still

relatively limited, and they are not sufficient to cover various clinical subtypes such as different

age groups, language backgrounds, and voice disorders. Therefore, the generalization ability

of the model still needs to be verified through larger-scale, multi-center clinical data.
